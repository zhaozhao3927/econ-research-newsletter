Contest or Contract: Assessing the Effectiveness of Contests in Online Labor Markets

Authors: Jason Chan, Jiahui Mo, Tingting Nian, Nila Zhang
ManagementScience | 2026-10-29 | 10.1287/mnsc.2024.07082

Abstract: Online labor markets have become increasingly popular among companies seeking global access to workers. Some platforms have adopted a contest-based scheme, allowing employers to review multiple submissions before selecting the best one. This practice raises a crucial question: Given a predefined budget, should employers choose a contest or contract scheme for tasks? This study examines the comparative effectiveness of contests versus contracts in achieving better job outcomes, focusing on design tasks characterized by high uncertainty. Using a comprehensive data set from a leading online labor market, we employ multiple empirical strategies, combined with machine learning and text mining techniques, to examine the impact of compensation schemes on job quality. Our results suggest that contract-based jobs yield better performance than contests in the context of ideation tasks. Further analysis, such as examining image similarity among pairwise solutions within a task, identifies “copying” behaviors as a key mechanism undermining the effectiveness of contests. In the public setting of contests, workers can observe others’ submissions and revise their own, leading to imitation rather than innovation. As competition intensity increases, workers have weaker incentives to exert effort, reducing solution quality. Consequently, the negative incentive effects in contests outweigh both the potential positive incentive and parallel effects. Moreover, although information availability can enhance work quality, contests inherently offer limited informational advantages compared with contracts. Taken together, our findings suggest that contract-based schemes are more effective than contests for design tasks, offering important implications for both employers and platform designers in online labor markets. This paper was accepted by Karan Girotra, operations management. Funding: N. Zhang acknowledges the support from the National Natural Science Foundation of China [Grant 72302053]. Supplemental Material: The online appendices and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.07082 .

Inflexibility and Leverage

Authors: Lifeng Gu, Dirk Hackbarth, Yuxin Luo
ManagementScience | 2026-10-29 | 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08672

Abstract: Firms’ inflexibility to adjust their scale persistently explains capital structure variations in a comprehensive sample and randomly selected subsamples. Higher inflexibility leads to lower financial leverage, potentially due to higher default risk and lower value of tax shields. Contraction inflexibility determines leverage more than expansion inflexibility. Moreover, our inflexibility measure explains financial leverage significantly better than other variables that potentially capture certain aspects of adjustment costs for capital or labor. In addition, inflexible firms increase leverage more following a positive credit supply shock. Analyses employing instrumental variables estimation confirm our main finding. Our results suggest inflexibility shapes corporate financial policies. This paper was accepted by Bo Becker, finance. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.08672 .

Multitype Information Acquisition in a Strategic Trading Model

Authors: Hui Ou-Yang, Weili Wu, Xingtan Zhang
ManagementScience | 2026-10-29 | 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08463

Abstract: We analyze information acquisition in a strategic trading model with multidimensional uncertainties. Our main result is that the equilibrium can exhibit multiplicity because of the integer nature of the number of informed traders and the strategic interactions across different types of informed traders. We show that the number of equilibria can be arbitrarily large. Moreover, asymmetric equilibria can arise even in symmetric settings. As information costs rise, fewer traders acquire information, yet prices can become more informative. These results shed light on the instability of financial markets and yield testable empirical predictions. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. Willingness to Compete in Dirty Competitions

Authors: Thomas Buser, Sahar Sangi
ManagementScience | 2026-10-29 | 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01218

Abstract: Competitive environments often leave room for “dirty” practices such as sabotage, retaliation, or dishonesty. We use an online experiment to document aggregate levels and individual differences in the willingness to engage in dirty competition and in the willingness to enter competitions where the opponent may play dirty. We then use the experimental data to validate a set of survey questions that capture willingness to engage in dirty competition above general willingness to compete. We elicit these questions in a representative survey panel and show that willingness to engage in dirty competition is a strong predictor of holding a management or supervisory position and of working in the private—versus the public—sector, but also of worse self-esteem, worse social relationships, and increased feelings of guilt and shame. Men, younger people, and lower-educated people are on average more willing to engage in dirty competition. This paper was accepted by Dorothea Kübler, behavioral economics and decision analysis. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.01218 .

Estimating the Pricing Kernel: A Simple Regression-Based Dimension-Reduction Approach

Authors: Bin Luo, Guofu Zhou, Ti Zhou
ManagementScience | 2026-10-28 | 10.1287/mnsc.2025.03608

Abstract: We propose a simple framework for estimating the pricing kernel in factor space by constructing the mean–variance efficient factor portfolio via an ordinary least squares formulation with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regularization. The method accommodates high-dimensional settings in which the number of candidate factors exceeds the sample size and automatically shrinks redundant factor weights toward zero. We show theoretically that the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of the estimated portfolio converges to the theoretical maximum at the fastest rate among existing regression-based approaches. Applying the method to 174 characteristic-based factors together with the market factor, we obtain an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 3.59, substantially outperforming competing strategies and delivering the smallest aggregate pricing errors. The selected factors vary over time in both composition and number, averaging about 50. We further develop a model comparison test and reject the hypothesis that eight widely used sparse factor models are sufficient to span the pricing kernel. This paper was accepted by Kay Giesecke, finance. Funding: This work was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China [Grants 72671091, 72371079, and 72501192, and Key Program 72432005] and the Science and Technology Planning Project of Guangdong Province [Grant 2023B1515020045]. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03608 .

From Employer Responsibility to Environmental Irresponsibility: Unintended Impacts of Social Insurance Law on Pollution Emissions

Authors: Yanan Li, Yu Qin, Wenjun Wang
ManagementScience | 2026-10-28 | 10.1287/mnsc.2025.00714

Abstract: This study investigates the unintended effect of the introduction of the 2011 Social Insurance Law on corporate pollution emissions. Using a difference-in-differences framework, our analysis reveals that enhanced social insurance contributions lead to higher industrial pollution emissions, as measured by chemical oxygen demand and wastewater emissions. The mechanism analysis indicates that the enforcement of the law increases firms’ labor costs and financial distress, motivating them to reduce efforts on end-of-pipe treatment activities and measures taken during the production process, which results in higher pollution emission intensity. Our study highlights the potential conflict between employee protection and corporate environmental goals. Further analysis suggests that stronger environmental monitoring and environmental, social, and governance disclosure requirements can partially mitigate this tradeoff, providing valuable insights for policymakers. Robust Auction Design with Support Information

Authors: Jerry Anunrojwong, Santiago R. Balseiro, Omar Besbes
ManagementScience | 2026-10-28 | 10.1287/mnsc.2023.02703

Abstract: A seller wants to sell an item to n buyers. Buyer valuations are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to a distribution unknown to the seller; the seller only knows that the support is included in [a, b]. To be robust, the seller chooses a dominant strategy incentive compatible mechanism that optimizes the worst-case performance relative to the ideal expected revenue the seller could have collected with knowledge of buyers’ valuations. Our analysis unifies the regret and the ratio objectives. For these objectives, we derive an optimal mechanism and the corresponding performance in quasi-closed form, as a function of the support information [a, b] and the number of buyers n. Our analysis reveals three regimes of support information and a new class of robust mechanisms. (i) When a/b is below a threshold, the optimal mechanism is a second-price auction (SPA) with random reserve, a focal class in earlier literature. (ii) When a/b is above another threshold, SPAs are strictly suboptimal, and an optimal mechanism belongs to a class of mechanisms we introduce, which we call pooling auctions (POOL); whenever the highest value is above a threshold, the mechanism still allocates to the highest bidder, but otherwise the mechanism allocates to a uniformly random buyer, that is, pools low types. (iii) When a/b is between two thresholds, a randomization between SPA and POOL is optimal. We also characterize optimal mechanisms within nested central subclasses of mechanisms: standard mechanisms that only allocate to the highest bidder, SPA with random reserve, and SPA with no reserve. We show strict separations in terms of performance across classes, implying that deviating from standard mechanisms is necessary for robustness. Lastly, we show that the same results hold under other distribution classes that capture “positive dependence,” namely i.i.d., mixture of i.i.d., and exchangeable and affiliated distributions, as well as i.i.d. regular distributions. This paper was accepted by David Simchi-Levi, revenue management and market analytics. Supplemental Material: The online appendices are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.02703 .

Navigating the Rugged Data Landscape: The Impact of Data-Extrapolation Technologies on Knowledge Production

Authors: Soomi Kim
ManagementScience | 2026-10-27 | 10.1287/mnsc.2023.04115

Abstract: Data-extrapolation technologies enable progress by importing and applying empirical patterns from one domain to another. Their reliance on empirical patterns, however, is also their limitation: these tools can only be applied where data are abundant. Using the setting of structural biology, I investigate the impact of a data-extrapolation technology on innovation. I exploit the variation in prior data availability to compare areas in which the tool was usable to those in which it was not. I find that the tool increased the quantity of research in data-rich areas: the resulting output yielded limited biological insights but was methodologically rigorous and contributed to downstream research. This paper was accepted by Toby Stuart, entrepreneurship and innovation. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.04115 .

Selection into Financial Education and Effects on Portfolio Choice

Authors: Irina Gemmo, Pierre-Carl Michaud, Olivia S. Mitchell
ManagementScience | 2026-10-27 | 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08268

Abstract: We study the impact of a financial education program on portfolio choices in a setting that allows us to examine both participation decisions and differences in program benefits across individuals. We directly elicit willingness to pay for financial education and show that individuals with higher financial literacy, greater confidence in applying new knowledge, and higher expected returns are willing to pay more. Using incentivized portfolio allocation tasks, we develop outcome measures tailored to individual risk preferences and show that financial education improves decision quality along these dimensions. The program increases the share of participants who experience an improvement by 20 percentage points, corresponding to average gains of 3.2 percentage points of initial wealth. Individuals with the highest willingness to participate benefit disproportionately from the program, with important implications for the design and evaluation of financial education initiatives. By isolating a specific investment skill in a setting with voluntary participation, our design sheds light on how selection into financial education shapes measured program effects and informs the assessment of broader workplace and public training programs. This paper was accepted by Marie Claire Villeval, behavioral economics and decision analysis. Funding: The authors thank the Pension Research Council of Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania, as well as the Retirement and Savings Institute at HEC Montréal, for generous research funding. Supplemental Material: The online appendices and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.08268 .

Corporate Pension Risk-Taking in a Low Interest Rate Environment

Authors: Vasso Ioannidou, Roberto Pinto, Zexi Wang
ManagementScience | 2026-10-26 | 10.1287/mnsc.2025.02928

Abstract: We examine how funding deficit pressures shape the risk-taking of U.S. corporate defined-benefit pension plans and how targeted regulatory relief can mitigate these incentives. Using the 2012 Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act reform, which temporarily eased funding pressure by revising discount rate calculations, we show that plans receiving greater relief significantly reduced their equity allocations and shifted toward safer assets. This derisking was most pronounced among active plans and those sponsored by more financially constrained firms. Our findings demonstrate that targeted regulatory relief can curb risk-taking incentives even without altering underlying economic conditions or financial reporting standards and shed light on the likely (and unlikely) frictions driving such behavior. This paper was accepted by Bo Becker, finance. Funding: R. Pinto acknowledges financial support from the Lancaster University Management School Pump Prime Program. The Equity Market Implications of the Retail Investment Boom

Authors: Philippe van der Beck, Cameron Cohen, Coralie Jaunin
ManagementScience | 2026-10-26 | 10.1287/mnsc.2025.04854

Abstract: We quantify the equilibrium impact of retail traders on the U.S. equity market within a structural model of asset demand. Estimating investor-specific demand curves, we find that Robinhood traders are fundamentally distinct from all other investors, including other households: their demand is the most elastic and tilts most strongly toward volatile and microcap stocks. Each dollar invested through Robinhood generates more than five dollars in aggregate equity repricing, exceeding hedge funds, other households, and all other investor types. We decompose the cross-sectional variation in stock returns during Robinhood’s growth surge in 2020 into investor-specific components and find that shifts in Robinhood traders’ latent demand drove 11% of small-cap return volatility despite their negligible aggregate wealth share. To explain these demand shifts, we show that Robinhood traders are the only investor group whose latent demand is well explained by attention proxies: their demand exhibits a V-shaped response to past returns and rises sharply with trading volume. Our findings suggest that the growth of retail trading platforms represents a fundamental shift in the composition of equity demand with equilibrium consequences disproportionate to retail investors’ share of aggregate wealth. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. Funding: P. van der Beck acknowledges financial support from the Swiss National Science Foundation. Supplemental Material: The data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.04854 .
